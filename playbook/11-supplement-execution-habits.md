# 11. SUPPLEMENT: Execution habits (NOT the course)
Source: ideas adapted from a publicly hosted internal operations guide from a video-production company (see SOURCES.md). It is about video production, not sales, and the evidence is anecdotal.
Everything here is paraphrased and translated to selling. Skipped on purpose: its "rank and remove people" culture and its extreme-hours mindset. Where this conflicts with the course (curious, not interrogating; meet buyers where they are), the course wins.

## 1. Name the bottleneck, then check in
When a deal step depends on one person (buyer or colleague), do not say "let me know when it's done." Say who the bottleneck is and why, in plain words, and agree a date.
- Script: "[Name], you're the one step between us and [outcome]. I can't [next thing] until [their step] is done. Can we agree on a date, and is it OK if I check in on [day/cadence]?"
- Then actually check in at the agreed cadence. A calendar reminder five days out is not a check-in. Ask about progress, not just "any update?"
- Every MAP step has exactly one owner. Two owners means no owner.

## 2. Critical components and backups
A critical component is something that, if it fails, there is no deal (signer, budget line, legal/security review, a required integration, the champion). List them per deal.
- For each: who owns it, what proves it is on track, and a backup path (second contact, alternate budget, fallback timeline).
- Raise problems the moment you see them, and bring your manager in earlier rather than later.
- Don't coin-flip a critical component: if it is 50/50, work another route in parallel.

## 3. The first minute and the midpoint
People leave early. Spend your best material at the start and re-engage in the middle.
- Cold call: the first 30 seconds earn the next 2 minutes. Lead with their world, not yours (course script first).
- Meeting or demo: show, don't tell. Skip long previews of what you will cover. Around the midpoint, re-engage with something specific to them (a number they gave, a result a similar customer saw, if real and in profile.md).
- Don't signal the end early. Close on a clear next step.

## 4. Match the promise
The subject line, opener or invite sets an expectation. The meeting must deliver exactly that. If you promised "15 minutes on missed appointments," do not turn it into a 40-minute product tour. Mismatch costs trust.

## 5. Ask with options, not a bare question
When you need a decision from a manager, exec, champion or anyone senior: bring the context, two or three options, your recommendation, and what you already checked.
- Weak: "What do you think of this discount?"
- Strong: "We're at $X. The buyer asked for Y. I see three options: (a) hold with a smaller scope, (b) trade term length for price, (c) walk. I recommend (b) because [reason]. Anything I'm missing?"

## 6. Say the negatives
Report why a deal might not close, not only why it will. Every forecast line and deal log has a "what could kill this" entry. Ask the buyer the same: "What would make you say no?"

## 7. Communication ladder
The more complex or at risk the message, the higher the form: call or in person beats text beats email. Written messages don't count as communicated until the reader confirms. End key emails with a clear request to confirm, and escalate to a call if there is no reply in 1-2 business days.

## 8. Is the "no" real?
One no from one person is data, not a verdict. Before closing a lead out: is it the right person, the right moment, the right framing? Try one different route (another stakeholder, a referral, a later date). Stop once a decision-maker clearly declines. This never means badgering anyone; stay curious.

## 9. Pipeline hygiene
- Work several deals every day. Going all-in on one deal for days lets the rest of the pipeline go stale.
- Keep a short priority list (2-3 items). Nothing jumps the list without a reason.
- Verify what sounds too good ("we'll sign Friday," "unlimited budget"): ask what changed and who confirmed it.

## 10. Small habits
- Ask an expert before reinventing: your SE, a customer, a former rep.
- Build slack into close dates for the steps you don't control.
- When price is the objection, look for creative trades before a discount (scope, term, timing, reference value; see playbook 09).
- After a lost deal, write a 5-line post-mortem: what happened, the earliest warning sign, what you would do differently.
- Compare a call to your last nine calls, not to a perfect ideal.
- Read and watch what your buyers read and watch (their industry news, their social feeds).
